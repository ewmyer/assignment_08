My approach was to build the pipeline in stages: load and join the data, add
geographic features, classify each visit, aggregate the results, and then display
them in a dashboard. The spatial join taught me to distinguish coordinates from
map geometry: point-in-polygon logic assigned Home and Work coordinates to a ZIP
polygon, while adding ZIPs to a DataFrame did not change the original data. I used
a coordinate reference system and a meters projection before taking polygon
centroids, because centroid and haversine calculations need a projection in which
distance has consistent units rather than raw degrees.

The time-only baseline gave me a useful starting point, but it could not account
for place. I used feature engineering to add d_home, d_work, and detour, then
improved the rule-based classification with named constants for near-work,
near-home, and commute detours. The most difficult lesson was ordering the rules:
an evening commute that is also near Work must be classified before the general
work-break rule. Local tests were especially useful for catching an incorrect
condition-to-label mapping and for verifying that all five reasons appear. Live
survey validation could not be run because a CENT API key was not available, so I
could not honestly verify agreement against the hidden validation set or tune by
the score table.

The tables and charts showed how pandas structure feeds visualization. pivot_table
and reindex made complete heatmaps with zero-filled hours and days.
groupby/agg produced monthly sales and ZIP demand, and np.select created anchor
ZIPs for workday and near-home visits. In the dashboard, a seaborn heatmap showed
visit density, a violin plot showed spending distributions, a Plotly trend
supported hover inspection, Folium markers showed shop locations with popups and
revenue-scaled radii, and choropleths showed GeoJSON polygons by member count,
demand, or site score.

The "where members live" map points to Syracuse ZIPs rather than every member's
true residence. That is why it can look as though it is pointing to the wrong
place: only members whose Home coordinates fall inside one of the supplied
Syracuse ZIP polygons are shown. Members living in Clay provide an important
example. Their coordinates are outside this ZIP set, so they are left out of
members_by_zip and make no member-count or demand contribution. The map therefore
shows where demand is observable in the included ZIPs, not every customer's full
location or population.

This data cannot tell us the reason for a future order, the age or household
income of a member, travel time by road, foot traffic, rent, parking, opening
hours, or whether customers are willing to cross from another area. It also
cannot tell us Clay demand in the absence of a Clay ZIP polygon or an unobserved
survey response in the validation set. The site score should be treated as a
decision aid, not a complete market analysis. Next time I would gather ZIP
coverage for Clay, survey a larger representative sample, compare road distance
and travel time with haversine distance, and test alternative thresholds before
deploying the dashboard.
